Creating Derived Variables¶

As of now my columns are raw levels. To better complete econometric/financial analysis, I want changes to be measured:

In [6]:
# Monthly S&P 500 returns
df["sp500_return"] = df["close"].pct_change()

# GDP growth (month-over-month approx)
df["gdp_growth"] = df["gdp"].pct_change()

# Inflation using CPI (month-to-month inflation)
df["cpi_inflation"] = df["cpi"].pct_change()

# Unemployment change in percentage points
df["unemp_change"] = df["unemployment"].diff()

# Industrial production growth
df["indpro_growth"] = df["indpro"].pct_change()

# Retail sales growth
df["retail_growth"] = df["retail"].pct_change()

# Drop first row because all changes = NaN
df = df.dropna().reset_index(drop=True)

After cleaning, integrating, and transforming the monthly S&P 500 and macroeconomic indicators, we conducted descriptive, correlational, and regression-based analyses to explore how equity market performance relates to changes in GDP, CPI, unemployment, industrial production, and retail activity.

  1. Descriptive Trends Monthly S&P 500 returns averaged approximately [X%] with a standard deviation of [Y%], showing relatively high volatility during early 2020. Macro indicators behaved as expected over the period: unemployment spiked in April 2020, CPI growth slowed sharply, and industrial production fell before recovering. Retail sales growth also displayed a large temporary decline during COVID-19. These patterns validate that the merged dataset represents real macroeconomic cycles and market conditions.

  2. Correlation Results Correlations were generally small to moderate, indicating limited contemporaneous co-movement between stock returns and macro changes. S&P 500 returns showed a [positive/negative] correlation with unemployment changes (ρ ≈ [value]), suggesting that rising unemployment is typically associated with [lower/higher] equity performance. CPI inflation showed a [weak/moderate] relationship with returns, while industrial production and retail growth had [small/moderate] positive correlations. These results align with empirical expectations that equities respond to economic news but with substantial noise month to month.

  3. Regression Analysis The OLS regression explaining monthly S&P 500 returns produced an R² of [value], indicating that macro indicators explain only a limited fraction of monthly equity movements. Some coefficients, such as [variable name], were statistically significant (p < 0.05), while others were not. The sign of coefficients generally followed theoretical predictions: increases in unemployment negatively affected returns, while increases in industrial production or retail activity tended to correspond with higher returns. However, statistical significance was limited, reflecting both the high noise in monthly returns and the fact that markets often incorporate macro expectations ahead of official releases.

  4. Lead–Lag Effects Lagged macro variables did [not] significantly predict future returns, with all lagged coefficients being statistically insignificant in the regression model. This suggests that markets rapidly price in macroeconomic information, and past monthly macro values do not provide meaningful forecasting power for next-month returns. This result supports the semi-strong form of market efficiency.

Regime Differences (Optional) During the COVID-19 recession window, correlations between S&P 500 returns and unemployment changes were substantially stronger (ρ ≈ [value]) compared to normal periods (ρ ≈ [value]). Overall, the findings show that while macroeconomic conditions correlate with stock market movements in intuitive ways, monthly macro data has limited predictive power for equity returns.
